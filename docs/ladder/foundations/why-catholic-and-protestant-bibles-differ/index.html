Why do Catholic and Protestant Bibles have different books?

By the 9nth Dimension team · Published November 25, 2026 · Last updated November 25, 2026

Catholic Bibles include seven Old Testament books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and 1–2 Maccabees) plus extra chapters of Esther and Daniel. Protestants set these aside during the Reformation, following the shorter Hebrew Scriptures of Judaism. Catholics kept the longer list found in the Greek Old Testament most early Christians read.

Which books are actually different?

Open a Catholic Bible and a Protestant one side by side and the New Testaments match exactly: the same 27 books. The difference sits entirely in the Old Testament. Protestant Bibles have 39 Old Testament books; Catholic Bibles have 46.

The seven extra books are Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees. Catholic Bibles also carry Greek additions to Esther and three additions to Daniel: the Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three Young Men, Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon.

Catholics call these books deuterocanonical, meaning "second canon." The term was coined in the sixteenth century and refers to when they were firmly recognized, not to a lower rank. Protestants usually call them the Apocrypha. Same books, two names, and the names tell you which side of the argument you grew up on.

Where did the extra books come from?

The story begins before Jesus. Many Jews lived outside Judea, especially in Alexandria, and spoke Greek. Over the last centuries BC, Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek. That translation, the Septuagint, circulated alongside other Jewish religious writings composed in the same era, some in Hebrew or Aramaic and some in Greek.

Some of those writings are well grounded in Jewish life. The prologue to Sirach, written by the author's grandson, says he translated his grandfather's Hebrew book into Greek after arriving in Egypt. Fragments of Sirach and of Tobit turned up among the Dead Sea Scrolls, so these were books Jews were copying and reading.

The first Christians, writing in Greek for Greek-speaking churches, mostly read their Old Testament in Greek. The great Christian Bible manuscripts of the fourth and fifth centuries include many of these books, though no two manuscripts carry exactly the same set.

Did the early church accept them?

Opinion was divided, and that division is the root of today's difference.

Jerome, who translated the Bible into Latin around AD 400, preferred the Hebrew Bible. In his preface to Samuel and Kings he said books outside the Hebrew collection should be set apart from the canon. Augustine, his contemporary, accepted the longer list. So did regional councils in North Africa, at Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397. For roughly a thousand years after that, the Western church read the longer Old Testament, while some scholars kept repeating Jerome's caution.

Jewish tradition went the other way. Josephus, the first-century Jewish historian (a non-canonical source), wrote of 22 sacred books, a number most scholars match to the Hebrew Bible Protestants use today, counted in a different arrangement. He also said writings after the Persian king Artaxerxes were not trusted in the same way. Rabbinic Judaism settled on that shorter collection, though scholars still debate exactly when it was closed.

What happened in the Reformation?

The question went from academic to explosive. One flashpoint was 2 Maccabees 12:43–45, where Judas Maccabeus sends an offering to Jerusalem on behalf of fallen soldiers. Catholic teachers cited it in support of prayer for the dead; Reformers who rejected purgatory challenged the book's authority.

Martin Luther's complete German Bible of 1534 moved these books into a separate section between the Testaments. Its heading called them books not equal to Holy Scripture yet useful and good to read. The Catholic Council of Trent answered in 1546 by formally defining the longer list as canonical, without distinction.

Other Protestants took different tones. The Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles say the church reads these books for example of life, but not to establish doctrine. The Westminster Confession of 1646 went further, saying they are no part of the canon. The 1611 King James Version still printed them between the Testaments. Over the following two centuries, Protestant printers and Bible societies increasingly dropped them, and most Protestants today have never seen them bound in their own Bibles.

Who has the stronger case?

Each side makes serious arguments, and you deserve to hear them fairly.

The Protestant case: Jesus and the apostles were Jews, and the Jewish Scriptures of their day point to the shorter collection. The New Testament quotes the Old Testament constantly but never quotes a deuterocanonical book with "it is written." Jerome, the church's greatest biblical scholar of his age, drew the line in the same place.

The Catholic case: the apostles' churches read the Greek Old Testament, and these books came with it. New Testament writers knew them. John 10:22 places Jesus at the Feast of Dedication, the festival whose origin is told in 1 Maccabees 4:52–59. Many scholars hear 2 Maccabees 7 behind Hebrews 11:35, on those who refused release to gain a better resurrection. Councils accepted the books long before the Reformation.

A skeptical historian will add that neither side's picture is tidy. Before the second century, Jewish and Christian collections were probably more fluid than either tradition later claimed, and Greek manuscripts never agreed on one list. On that reading, Trent and the Reformers each made formal a boundary that had been blurry for centuries.

Today's practice

Read the books most of us were never handed, and see where the New Testament touches them. Any Catholic Bible, or an edition "with Apocrypha," will have them.

  1. Read John 10:22–23. Then read 1 Maccabees 4:52–59. Write down what the feast commemorates and how long it lasted.
  2. Read Hebrews 11:35. Then read 2 Maccabees 7, the mother and her seven sons. Note any phrases about resurrection that sound alike.
  3. Read 2 Maccabees 12:43–45. Ask yourself why this short passage became a Reformation battleground.
  4. Read the short prologue to Sirach. Note what the translator says about putting Hebrew into Greek.

Why does this matter for your faith?

Because "the Bible" has always had slightly different edges in different churches, and knowing why lets you read your own Bible with open eyes rather than suspicion. A Protestant can read Maccabees as history that fills the four-century gap between Malachi and Matthew. A Catholic can see why her Protestant friends hold the line where Jerome did. Frequently asked questions

How many books are in a Catholic Bible versus a Protestant Bible?

A Catholic Bible has 73 books and a Protestant Bible has 66. The New Testament is the same 27 in both. The difference is seven Old Testament books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and 1–2 Maccabees) plus longer versions of Esther and Daniel.

Does the Eastern Orthodox Bible have even more books?

Yes. Orthodox churches generally accept the Catholic deuterocanonicals and add others from the Greek Old Testament, such as 1 Esdras, the Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151 and 3 Maccabees. The exact list varies a little between Orthodox churches.

Did the original King James Version include the Apocrypha?

Yes. The 1611 King James Version printed the Apocrypha in a separate section between the Old and New Testaments. Later Protestant printings increasingly left that section out, which is why most Protestants today have never seen it in their own Bibles.

Is it wrong for a Protestant to read Tobit or Maccabees?

Not according to the early Protestant tradition itself. Luther called these books useful and good to read, and the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles say they are read for example of life, though not to establish doctrine. Many Protestants read them today as history and devotional background.
