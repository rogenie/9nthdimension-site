What is Isaiah 53 about? The suffering servant and how it's read

By the 9nth Dimension team · Published November 22, 2026 · Last updated November 22, 2026

Isaiah 53 is the climax of a poem, beginning at Isaiah 52:13, about a servant of God who is despised, wounded and killed, yet bears the sins of others and is finally lifted up. Christians have read it as a portrait of Jesus since the New Testament. Jewish tradition usually reads the servant as the people of Israel.

What does Isaiah 53 actually say?

The poem opens with God speaking: "my servant will act wisely" and be "raised and lifted up" (Isaiah 52:13). Then the scene turns. The servant's appearance is marred beyond recognition (52:14), and kings are left speechless (52:15).

In chapter 53 the voice changes to "we." A group confesses that they despised this servant and thought God was punishing him (53:3–4). Then comes the turn that makes the passage famous: "he was pierced for our transgressions" (53:5), and "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (53:6).

The servant goes silent "like a lamb to the slaughter" (53:7), is cut off from the land of the living (53:8), and is buried "with the wicked" and "with the rich" (53:9). Yet the poem does not end at the grave. He "will see his offspring" and "prolong his days" (53:10), and God gives him a portion with the great (53:12).

Notice where the poem begins. The chapter break between 52 and 53 was added in the Middle Ages. The poem itself starts three verses earlier, and reading it whole changes how it feels: it opens and closes with exaltation, with the suffering in the middle.

Who is the suffering servant in Isaiah?

This is one of the oldest debates in biblical interpretation, and it is in the Bible itself. In Acts 8:34, an Ethiopian official reading this passage asks Philip: is the prophet talking about himself or someone else? Here are the main answers.

Jesus of Nazareth. This is the classic Christian reading. Jesus quotes Isaiah 53:12, "he was numbered with the transgressors," as being fulfilled in him (Luke 22:37). Philip starts from this passage to tell the Ethiopian about Jesus (Acts 8:35). First Peter 2:24 borrows its language: "by his wounds you have been healed." Christians also point to the silent servant, the death for others, the grave with a rich man (compare Matthew 27:57–60), and life after death.

The people of Israel. This is the most common Jewish reading, and it has textual support. Isaiah repeatedly names Israel as God's servant: "you, Israel, my servant" (Isaiah 41:8), and "you are my servant, Israel" (49:3). On this view, the "we" of chapter 53 are the nations, finally realizing that suffering Israel was not cursed. The medieval rabbi Rashi read it this way, and the church father Origen (non-canonical) records a Jewish teacher making the same argument in the third century.

An individual from Isaiah's own time. Many modern scholars argue that the servant was a figure the first audience would have recognized: the prophet himself, a faithful group within Israel, or an exiled leader. Jeremiah describes himself in similar words, "like a gentle lamb led to the slaughter" (Jeremiah 11:19).

A future Messiah. Some ancient Jewish sources also read the passage messianically. The Aramaic Targum of Isaiah (non-canonical) opens the poem with "my servant the Messiah," though it reshapes the suffering so it mostly falls on others. The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b, also non-canonical) links Isaiah 53:4 to the Messiah.

How do Christians and skeptics argue over Isaiah 53?

A skeptic will say the New Testament writers knew the passage and told Jesus' story in its language, so the match is not surprising. They also point to the Israel verses in Isaiah 49:3 and 41:8 as strong evidence for the national reading.

A Christian reader can answer that the servant in Isaiah 49:5–6 has a mission to Israel, to restore Jacob and bring him back to God, which is hard to say of Israel itself. The servant in 53:9 is also said to have done no violence and spoken no deceit, while Isaiah elsewhere is blunt about Israel's sins. Some Christian scholars hold both readings together: the servant is Israel, and Jesus is the one faithful Israelite who fulfils that calling.

Fair readers on both sides agree on one thing: the passage is about innocent suffering that turns out to be for others. They differ on who it describes and whether Isaiah was looking forward to a specific person.

Does the wording of Isaiah 53 differ between Bibles?

In a few places, yes. Isaiah 52:15 reads "so he will sprinkle many nations" in the NIV and "so he shall startle many nations" in the NRSV, because the Hebrew word is uncertain. In 53:11, the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran and the Septuagint add the word "light," which some translations now include. We cover that scroll in our article on the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Today's practice

Read the poem the way its first readers did, as one piece.

  1. Read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 in one sitting, ignoring the chapter break. Mark every "he" in one color and every "we" or "our" in another.
  2. Read Isaiah 41:8 and 49:3, then 49:5–6. Write one sentence on how the servant can be called Israel and also be sent to Israel.
  3. Read Acts 8:30–35. Write down the Ethiopian's question in your own words, then your own answer, and why.
  4. Read 1 Peter 2:21–25. List the phrases Peter borrows from Isaiah 53.

Frequently asked questions

Where does the suffering servant passage start and end?

It runs from Isaiah 52:13 to 53:12. The chapter break was added in the Middle Ages, long after Isaiah was written, so reading chapter 53 alone cuts off the first three verses of the poem.

Does the New Testament quote Isaiah 53?

Yes, several times. Jesus applies Isaiah 53:12 to himself in Luke 22:37, Matthew 8:17 quotes 53:4, and Acts 8:32–35 shows Philip explaining 53:7–8 as being about Jesus. First Peter 2:22–25 also echoes the chapter closely.

Why do Jewish readers say the servant is Israel?

Because Isaiah itself calls Israel God's servant several times, including Isaiah 41:8 and 49:3. On this reading, the nations in chapter 53 speak about Israel's suffering among them. The medieval commentator Rashi is the best-known voice for this view.

Did any ancient Jewish sources read Isaiah 53 as the Messiah?

Some did. The Aramaic Targum of Isaiah calls the servant in 52:13 "my servant the Messiah," though it applies the suffering mostly to others. A passage in the Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b) also links Isaiah 53:4 to the Messiah. Both are non-canonical.

Was Isaiah 53 written by the prophet Isaiah?

That is debated. Traditional Jewish and Christian views hold that the eighth-century prophet Isaiah wrote the whole book. Many modern scholars assign chapters 40 to 55 to a later prophet writing during or after the Babylonian exile.
