**The Nephilim are a mysterious group named in Genesis 6:4 and Numbers 13:33. Genesis connects them to the time when the "sons of God" took human wives and calls them the mighty men of old. Whether they were the offspring of angels, of rulers, or simply powerful humans has been debated for over two thousand years.**

## What does Genesis 6 actually say?

The whole episode is four verses long, which is part of why it has generated so much speculation. Genesis 6:1–2 says that when people multiplied, the "sons of God" saw that the daughters of man were attractive and took wives from them. Verse 3 records God limiting man's days to 120 years. Verse 4 then says the Nephilim were on the earth in those days, "and also afterward," when the sons of God had children with human women, and it calls those children "mighty men" and "men of renown."

Then, with no further explanation, verse 5 turns to the wickedness that leads to the Flood.

Notice what the text does not say. It never describes the Nephilim's size. It never says outright that the Nephilim were the children of these unions, only that they were around at that time. And it never uses the word "angel." Every theory about the Nephilim is built on how you fill in those gaps.

## What does the word Nephilim mean?

No one is certain. Many scholars connect it to the Hebrew verb *naphal*, "to fall," which gives meanings like "fallen ones" or "those who fall upon others." Others propose different roots. The translation "giants" in the King James Version comes through the Greek Septuagint, which rendered the word *gigantes*.

The only other place the word appears in the Hebrew Bible is Numbers 13:33. The spies Moses sent into Canaan report seeing the Nephilim, linking them to the descendants of Anak, and say, "we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers." That verse, not Genesis, is where the image of towering giants really comes from.

## Who were the "sons of God"?

Your answer to this question decides who you think the Nephilim were. There are three major readings, and each has serious defenders.

**1. Angelic beings.** This is the oldest reading we can document. Elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, "sons of God" refers to heavenly beings who stand before God (Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7). The non-canonical book of 1 Enoch, written before Jesus, retells Genesis 6 with angels called Watchers who descend, take wives and father giants (1 Enoch 6–7). Jubilees, also non-canonical, tells a similar story. Many early church writers followed this reading. Supporters also point to Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4, which speak of angels who sinned and are kept in chains for judgment, as likely allusions to this tradition.

**2. The line of Seth.** In this reading, the "sons of God" are the godly descendants of Seth, and the "daughters of man" come from the line of Cain. The sin is a faithful family intermarrying with an unfaithful one. It is usually traced to Julius Africanus and was popularized by Augustine (*City of God*, book 15), and it became the dominant view in much of the church. Its defenders often cite Matthew 22:30, where Jesus says that in the resurrection people do not marry but are like angels in heaven, as a reason to doubt that angels could take wives. The other side replies that Jesus speaks of angels "in heaven," not ones who abandoned their place.

**3. Kings and rulers.** Some early Jewish interpreters, including the Aramaic Targums, read "sons of God" as "sons of the nobles" or "great ones." In this view, powerful rulers seized whatever women they wanted, and their violent offspring became the famous warriors of old. Supporters note that ancient Near Eastern kings were sometimes styled as sons of a god.

Many modern scholars add a fourth, more skeptical observation: that Genesis 6:1–4 preserves a fragment of an older story about divine beings and legendary heroes, similar to tales found elsewhere in the ancient world, which the biblical author retold briefly and set within the lead-up to the Flood. Christians who hold a high view of Scripture can engage that view fairly, since the text itself seems to expect readers to already know something of the story.

## How could there be Nephilim after the Flood?

This puzzle has pushed readers in several directions. Genesis 6:4 says the Nephilim were on earth "in those days, and also afterward," which some take as a signal that the same kind of beings appeared again later. Others read Numbers 13:33 more cautiously: Numbers 13:32 calls the spies' report a "bad report," and the spies were explaining why Israel should not enter the land. On that reading, "Nephilim" is what terrified men called people they could not defeat, the way we might call a rival team "monsters."

Deuteronomy also mentions tall peoples called the Anakim and Rephaim (Deuteronomy 2:10–11, 20–21) and says the king Og of Bashan was the last of the Rephaim (Deuteronomy 3:11). Those passages describe large and formidable peoples, but they do not call them Nephilim.

## Is there any physical evidence of giants?

No. No verified skeletons of giant humans have ever been excavated. The photographs that circulate online of enormous skeletons at dig sites are doctored images, not real finds. A faith anchored in Scripture does not need them, and sharing them hands skeptics an easy reason to dismiss everything else you say.

### Today's practice

Read the passages yourself before deciding which reading you find strongest.

1. Read **Genesis 6:1–8** slowly. Write down only what the text states, and circle every word you are tempted to read into it.
2. Read **Job 1:6** and **Job 38:7**. How is "sons of God" used there?
3. Read **Numbers 13:30–33**. Who is speaking, and why might they describe the land this way?
4. Read **Jude 6** and **2 Peter 2:4**. Then, if you want to see the tradition they may echo, read **1 Enoch 6–7** (non-canonical) in a public-domain translation.
5. Finish by writing one sentence: which reading do you find most convincing, and which verse moved you there?

## Why does this strange passage matter?

Genesis 6:1–4 shows that the Bible was written into a world that took the unseen realm seriously, and that the boundary between heaven and earth matters to God. Whichever reading you hold, the passage sets up the Flood as God's response to corruption that had spread through everything. It also explains why later books like Jude and 2 Peter could mention angels who sinned without stopping to explain.
