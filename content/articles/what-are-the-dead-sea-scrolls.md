**The Dead Sea Scrolls are roughly 900 ancient Jewish manuscripts found in caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea, beginning in 1947. Copied between about the third century BC and the first century AD, they include the oldest known copies of Old Testament books, and they show both how carefully Scripture was copied and where early copies differed.**

## How were the Dead Sea Scrolls found?

The first scrolls came out of a cave in the Judean desert in 1947, found by Bedouin shepherds near a ruined settlement called Qumran, on the northwest shore of the Dead Sea. Over the next decade, Bedouin and archaeologists searched the cliffs, and eleven caves near Qumran yielded scrolls, most broken into thousands of fragments.

The collection is written mostly in Hebrew, with some Aramaic and Greek. A few survived nearly whole; most had to be pieced together like a puzzle, which is partly why full publication took decades.

## What is actually in the Dead Sea Scrolls?

Think of it as an ancient library with three kinds of shelves:

- **Biblical manuscripts.** About a quarter of the scrolls are copies of books from the Hebrew Bible. Every book is represented except Esther. Psalms, Deuteronomy and Isaiah appear in the most copies.
- **Other Jewish writings of the era.** Here you meet books that most Christians have heard of but never read: Aramaic fragments of 1 Enoch, Hebrew copies of Jubilees, and books like Tobit and Sirach. 1 Enoch and Jubilees are non-canonical for Jews, Catholics, Eastern Orthodox and Protestants, though the Ethiopian Orthodox Church includes both. Tobit and Sirach are in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles but not in Jewish or Protestant ones.
- **The community's own texts.** Rules for a disciplined community (the Community Rule and the Damascus Document), a battle plan for the end of days (the War Scroll), and commentaries that read prophets like Habakkuk as if they spoke directly about the community's own time. None of these are Scripture for any church or synagogue.

## Who wrote and hid the scrolls?

This is where the debate starts. The leading view is that a Jewish group living at Qumran owned the scrolls and copied many of them. Many scholars identify that group with the Essenes, a strict Jewish movement known from the Jewish historians Josephus and Philo (both non-canonical sources writing in the first century AD) and the Roman author Pliny the Elder, who placed Essenes on the western side of the Dead Sea. Their own texts speak of a leader they called the Teacher of Righteousness.

Not everyone agrees. Some scholars argue the connection between the ruins and the caves is weaker than it looks, and that part or all of the collection came from Jerusalem and was hidden in the desert during the Jewish war with Rome (AD 66–70). Others accept a Qumran community but doubt the Essene label. What nearly everyone accepts is that the scrolls were hidden around the time of that war and stayed in the dark for almost nineteen centuries.

## How do the scrolls change what we know about the Old Testament?

Before 1947, the oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament were medieval, such as the Aleppo Codex from the 900s AD. The Great Isaiah Scroll, a complete copy of Isaiah from Cave 1, is generally dated to the second century BC. That is roughly a thousand years earlier.

Two things come out of comparing them, and both matter.

**The first is continuity.** The Great Isaiah Scroll differs from the later Hebrew text in many small spellings and wordings, yet it is plainly the same book, chapter after chapter. Many biblical scrolls stand close to the Masoretic Text, the Hebrew text behind most Old Testament translations today.

**The second is variety.** Not every copy matches. Some scrolls side with readings known from the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation. A few examples you can check in study Bibles and translation notes:

- **1 Samuel 17:4.** The standard Hebrew text makes Goliath six cubits and a span tall. A Samuel scroll from Cave 4 reads four cubits and a span, matching the Septuagint.
- **Deuteronomy 32:8.** The standard Hebrew text says God fixed the borders of the peoples by the number of "the sons of Israel." A Qumran copy reads "sons of God," which is why the ESV and several other translations now print that wording. It opens a door to the idea of a divine council.
- **Isaiah 53:11.** The Great Isaiah Scroll reads that the servant "will see light," matching the Septuagint. Translations such as the NIV and NRSV include the word "light."

A skeptic will point out that this variety means the text was not frozen before the first century. That is fair, and translators take it seriously. A believer can answer just as fairly: the differences are visible, catalogued and footnoted, and most are small. Scholars weigh these readings one by one rather than treating any single manuscript as perfect.

## Do the Dead Sea Scrolls connect to the New Testament?

No New Testament book is among them, and no scroll names Jesus. But they show the world the New Testament was born into. The Community Rule uses Isaiah 40:3, "prepare the way of the LORD in the wilderness," to explain why the community went out into the desert. All four Gospels use the same verse for John the Baptist (Matthew 3:3, Mark 1:3, Luke 3:4, John 1:23). Some scholars wonder whether John knew a group like this; others see only a shared verse. The scrolls cannot settle it.

They also show that intense hope for a Messiah and a sharp contrast between light and darkness were already alive in Jewish thought.

### Today's practice

Read Scripture the way a scholar reads the scrolls: with the footnotes.

1. Open a translation with full textual notes, such as the NET Bible (free online). Read **1 Samuel 17:4** and find the note on Goliath's height.
2. Read **Deuteronomy 32:8–9** in the ESV, then in the KJV. Write down how "sons of God" and "children of Israel" change the meaning of the verse.
3. Read **Isaiah 40:3**, then **Matthew 3:1–3**. Notice how the same verse could mean "go to the wilderness" for one group and "here is the one who prepares the way" for another.
4. Visit the Israel Museum's free online Great Isaiah Scroll and find **Isaiah 53**. You are looking at a copy written before Jesus was born.

## Why should a Christian care about the Dead Sea Scrolls?

Because they make the Bible's history touchable. You can see Isaiah as a scribe copied it before Christ, how faithfully it was passed on, and where copies differed. That is evidence you can examine for yourself, not a "just trust us."
