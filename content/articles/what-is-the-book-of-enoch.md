**The Book of Enoch, usually called 1 Enoch, is an ancient Jewish writing composed in Enoch's name between about the third century BC and the first century AD. Jude quotes it, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church counts it as Scripture, but Judaism and most churches never accepted it into their canon.**

## Who was Enoch in the Bible?

The Bible gives Enoch only a few lines, which is exactly why he fascinated ancient readers. Genesis lists him in the line from Adam to Noah, then says he "walked with God; and he was not, for God took him" (Genesis 5:24). Unlike everyone else in that chapter, there is no "and he died."

The New Testament remembers him twice. Hebrews 11:5 says he was taken up so that he did not see death. Jude 14 calls him "the seventh from Adam." A man who did not die and walked closely with God was the perfect figure for later writers to imagine as a receiver of heavenly secrets.

## What is actually in the Book of Enoch?

1 Enoch is not one book so much as a small library collected under one name. Scholars usually divide it into five parts:

- **The Book of the Watchers (chapters 1–36).** It retells Genesis 6:1–4, where the "sons of God" take human wives. Here they are angels called Watchers who descend, teach forbidden knowledge, and father giants. God then judges them.
- **The Parables, or Similitudes (37–71).** Visions of judgment centered on a heavenly figure called the Chosen One and the Son of Man.
- **The Astronomical Book (72–82).** A detailed scheme of the sun, moon and stars, built on a 364-day year.
- **The Dream Visions (83–90).** Includes the "Animal Apocalypse," which retells Israel's history with people pictured as animals.
- **The Epistle of Enoch (91–108).** Moral warnings about the righteous and the wicked, with a short apocalypse about the ages of history.

The sections were written at different times. The Book of the Watchers and the Astronomical Book are generally dated to the third century BC or earlier, which makes them some of the oldest Jewish writings outside the Hebrew Bible. The date of the Parables is debated, with most scholars placing it around the turn of the era.

## Does the Bible really quote the Book of Enoch?

Yes. Jude 14–15 says Enoch prophesied that the Lord comes with countless holy ones to execute judgment on the ungodly. That passage closely matches 1 Enoch 1:9, the opening of the Book of the Watchers. Almost all scholars, conservative and skeptical alike, agree that Jude is quoting it.

What that means is where readers part ways:

- **One reading** holds that Jude quotes a true line from a respected book without endorsing the whole book, much as Paul quotes Greek poets in Acts 17:28.
- **Some scholars** argue Jude treated 1 Enoch as real prophecy, and that some early Christians gave it near-scriptural weight.
- **Others** note that Jude 6, about angels who left their proper home and are kept in chains for judgment, and 2 Peter 2:4 seem to assume the Watchers story is familiar to their readers.

Either way, it tells you that 1 Enoch was well known among the first Christians, which is reason enough to know what it says.

## Why isn't the Book of Enoch in the Bible?

There was no single vote that threw it out. A few factors worked together:

1. **Judaism never included it.** The Hebrew scriptures that became the Jewish canon did not contain 1 Enoch, and most church canons of the Old Testament grew from Jewish collections.
2. **It is written in an ancient figure's name.** The church fathers who weighed it knew it was very hard to see a book from before the Flood surviving to their day. Augustine, in *City of God* (book 15), set it aside because its origin could not be trusted.
3. **Its reception was mixed.** Tertullian, writing around AD 200, defended it as Scripture, while others treated it with suspicion. Over time, Western and Eastern churches left it outside their lists.

The Ethiopian church took the other path. It kept 1 Enoch in its canon, and that is the main reason the book survives in full: the only complete text is in Ge'ez, the ancient language of Ethiopia. European scholars did not have it until Ethiopic manuscripts reached Europe in the late 1700s.

## What did the Dead Sea Scrolls reveal about Enoch?

Among the scrolls found near Qumran, scholars identified Aramaic fragments of 1 Enoch, covering every major section except the Parables. That told us two important things. The book was read and copied by Jews before Jesus was born, and the Ethiopic version descends from a genuinely ancient text rather than a medieval invention.

It also raises a fair question a skeptic will ask: if the community at Qumran valued Enoch, why did it not make the Jewish canon? The honest answer is that we do not know every reason. Many books were read in that era, and only some became Scripture for the wider community.

### Today's practice

Read the passages side by side and notice what each one adds.

1. Read **Genesis 5:21–24**. Write down everything the Bible actually tells you about Enoch.
2. Read **Jude 14–15**. Then find **1 Enoch 1:9** in a public-domain translation (R. H. Charles's is free online) and compare the wording.
3. Read **Genesis 6:1–4**, then **1 Enoch 6–7**. List what 1 Enoch adds: names, numbers, motives.
4. Ask yourself one question: which details come from Scripture, and which came from a later author filling in the gaps?

## How should a Christian read the Book of Enoch?

Read it the way you would read an ancient sermon or commentary: as a window into how Jews before and during Jesus' time imagined angels, judgment and the end of the age. It helps explain phrases in Jude and 2 Peter that otherwise feel abrupt. It is not a secret Bible that overrides the one you have, and the traditions that reject it are not hiding anything. They made a considered judgment over centuries.
