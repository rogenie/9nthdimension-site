**No single person, pope or council decided. The books of the Bible were recognized gradually, over about three centuries, as churches across the Roman world kept reading the same writings as Scripture. Church leaders and regional councils later wrote down and confirmed lists that already reflected wide use, and different traditions still differ on the Old Testament.**

## Is it true the Council of Nicaea chose the books?

It is one of the most repeated claims about the Bible, and it is not supported by the record. The Council of Nicaea met in AD 325 to settle a fight over whether the Son is fully God, and to address the date of Easter. Its creed and canons survive, and none of them lists biblical books.

So where did the canon come from? It helps to remember that the first Christians already had a Bible: the Jewish Scriptures. Jesus refers to "the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms" in Luke 24:44, a three-part shape that matches how the Hebrew Scriptures came to be arranged. The question for the early church was which of its own writings belonged beside them.

## How did the New Testament books get recognized?

Some of that recognition happens inside the New Testament itself. In 2 Peter 3:15–16, Paul's letters are placed alongside "the other Scriptures." In 1 Timothy 5:18, a line from Deuteronomy 25:4 is quoted as Scripture next to the saying "the worker deserves his wages," which matches Jesus' words in Luke 10:7.

By the late second century, the four Gospels, Acts and Paul's letters were widely read in worship. Pressure helped sharpen the lines. Around AD 140, a teacher named Marcion rejected the Old Testament and accepted only an edited Luke and ten of Paul's letters. Irenaeus of Lyon, writing around AD 180, argued that the church had exactly four Gospels, no more and no fewer.

A list called the Muratorian Fragment names most of our New Testament and says the Shepherd of Hermas may be read privately but not publicly in church. Most scholars date it to the late second century, while some argue for the fourth. Either way, it shows churches sorting books into categories.

## Which books were debated, and why?

This is the part most Sunday schools skip. In the early fourth century, the church historian Eusebius (a non-canonical source, like all the church fathers here) sorted books into those everyone accepted, those some disputed, and those he rejected. His disputed list included James, Jude, 2 Peter and 2 and 3 John. He also recorded divided opinion on Revelation.

Meanwhile, other books were loved and read. Codex Sinaiticus, a fourth-century Greek Bible, includes the Epistle of Barnabas and the Shepherd of Hermas after Revelation. Writings like the Didache and 1 Clement were treasured too.

Scholars usually describe the tests churches applied this way:

- **Apostolic connection.** Was it written by an apostle or someone close to one?
- **Agreement with the faith.** Did it fit the teaching handed down from the apostles?
- **Wide use.** Was it read in churches across many regions, not just one city?
- **Antiquity.** Did it come from the apostolic age, not later?

These were not applied like a checklist in a committee room. They describe how churches reasoned over generations.

## When did the list of 27 books appear?

In AD 367, Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, sent his churches an Easter letter listing exactly the 27 books of our New Testament. It is the earliest surviving list that matches ours. Regional councils in North Africa, at Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397, affirmed the same 27. Notice the direction: the councils confirmed what the churches were already reading. They did not invent a Bible.

## What about the Old Testament?

Here the traditions part ways. Josephus, the first-century Jewish historian (non-canonical), wrote of 22 sacred books, which most scholars match to the Hebrew Bible Protestants use today, counted differently. Christians, though, largely read the Old Testament in Greek, and Greek collections included extra books like Tobit, Judith, Sirach and 1–2 Maccabees.

Some church fathers, including Jerome, preferred the shorter Hebrew list. Others, including Augustine, accepted the longer one. In the Reformation, Luther's Bible of 1534 placed those books in a separate section as useful but not equal to Scripture. The Catholic Council of Trent in 1546 formally defined them as canonical. Eastern Orthodox churches accept a similar, slightly larger set, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church has a broader canon still, including 1 Enoch and Jubilees.

A skeptic will say this shows the Bible was assembled by human politics. That critique deserves a fair hearing: there were real arguments, and some lasted centuries. A believer can answer that the slowness is the point. No ruler imposed the list; the core New Testament was recognized across rival cities and languages before any council spoke. Both sides agree the process was long and human. They differ on whether God was at work in it.

### Today's practice

Watch the canon forming inside the New Testament itself.

1. Read **2 Peter 3:15–16**. Underline the phrase that puts Paul's letters next to "the other Scriptures."
2. Read **1 Timothy 5:18**, then **Deuteronomy 25:4** and **Luke 10:7**. Note which two sources the verse joins under the word "Scripture."
3. Read **Luke 24:44**. Write down the three parts of the Scriptures Jesus names.
4. Look up Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (free online in translation) and compare his New Testament list with the table of contents in your own Bible.

## Why does this matter for your faith?

Because it replaces a vague "the church decided" with a story you can examine. Knowing that Jude was once debated, or that Hermas was once read, does not have to unsettle you. It shows a church that took the question seriously.
