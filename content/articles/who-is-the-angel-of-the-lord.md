**The Angel of the LORD is a mysterious messenger in the Old Testament who speaks as God in the first person, receives worship-like fear, and is sometimes called God outright. Many Christians, from the early church onward, read him as the Son appearing before his birth; others read him as an envoy carrying God's name.**

## Where does the Angel of the LORD appear?

He shows up at some of the most intimate moments in the Old Testament, usually when someone is alone, afraid or at the edge of their strength.

He finds Hagar by a spring in the wilderness after she has fled from Sarai (Genesis 16:7–13). He calls from heaven to stop Abraham's hand on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:11–18). He appears to Moses "in a flame of fire" from the burning bush (Exodus 3:2). He blocks Balaam's road while the donkey sees him first (Numbers 22:22–35). He sits under an oak while Gideon threshes wheat in hiding (Judges 6:11–24), and he announces Samson's birth to Manoah and his wife (Judges 13).

The Hebrew word *mal'ak* simply means messenger. It can describe a human courier. So the word on its own does not settle who this is. What makes the figure strange is what he says.

## Why does the messenger talk like God?

Read the stories slowly and you notice the speaker keeps sliding from "the angel" to God himself.

To Hagar, the angel promises, "I will surely multiply your offspring" (Genesis 16:10), a promise only God can make. Then Hagar names "the LORD who spoke to her" as the God who sees her (16:13). On Moriah, the angel says Abraham has not withheld his son "from me," and then swears "by myself... declares the LORD" (Genesis 22:12, 16). At the bush, the angel appears in the flame, and then "God called to him out of the bush" and says, "I am the God of your father" (Exodus 3:4, 6). In Jacob's dream, the angel of God says, "I am the God of Bethel" (Genesis 31:11–13).

Two details push the question further. In Exodus 23:20–21, God promises to send an angel ahead of Israel and warns them to obey him, because "my name is in him." And when Manoah asks the messenger's name, he answers that it is "wonderful," then ascends in the altar flame. Manoah's conclusion: "We shall surely die, for we have seen God" (Judges 13:18–22).

## But is he also distinct from God?

Yes, and this is what keeps the puzzle honest. In Zechariah 1:12, the Angel of the LORD prays to the LORD, asking how long he will withhold mercy from Jerusalem. In Zechariah 3:1–2, Joshua the high priest stands before him while Satan accuses, and the rebuke comes as "The LORD rebuke you." In 2 Samuel 24:16, God tells the destroying angel to stay his hand.

So the text holds two things together: this figure speaks as God, and he also speaks *to* God. How you resolve that tension is where the readings divide.

## What are the main readings?

**He is the Son before Bethlehem.** Ancient tradition holds that these appearances were the pre-incarnate Word. Justin Martyr, a second-century church father (a non-canonical source), argued in his *Dialogue with Trypho* that the one who appeared to Abraham and Moses was distinct from the Father yet truly God. Many evangelical scholars today hold this view. They point to the figure's divine speech combined with his distinction from the LORD, and to John 1:18, which says no one has ever seen God and that the only Son has made him known.

**He is God's envoy who carries God's name.** On this reading, the angel is a created messenger so fully authorized that his words are God's words, the way an ambassador speaks for a king. Jewish interpreters have generally read the figure this way. Augustine, another church father, argued in *On the Trinity* that Old Testament appearances of God were generally made through angels, while still revealing the Lord.

**He is a self-manifestation of God, not a separate person.** Some scholars read the Angel as God himself appearing in visible form, with no claim about the Trinity drawn from the passages themselves.

**He was added to soften the stories.** Some critical scholars propose that in several passages the word "angel" was inserted by later editors, uneasy with stories of God appearing directly. On this view, the shifting speaker is a seam in the text, not a theological clue. Others find no clear evidence of such editing and read the stories as written.

Each reading takes the texts seriously. The Christ reading explains the divine speech best; the envoy reading explains the distinction best; the critical reading explains the seams. None of them makes every verse fit effortlessly.

### Today's practice

Read with a pencil and track who is speaking.

1. Read **Genesis 16:7–13**. Circle every "I" the angel uses. Note what Hagar calls the one who spoke to her in verse 13.
2. Read **Exodus 3:1–6**. Mark where the text says "the angel of the LORD" and where it switches to "God."
3. Read **Judges 13:15–22**. Write down Manoah's conclusion in verse 22 in your own words.
4. Read **Zechariah 1:12–13** and **3:1–2**. Note where the Angel speaks *to* the LORD, and who rebukes Satan in 3:2.

Then ask: which reading above best explains all four passages? Which verse resists it most?

## What does this mean for walking with God?

Look at who the Angel finds. A pregnant servant girl running from harsh treatment into the desert. A father about to lose his son. A fugitive shepherd. A frightened farmer hiding his harvest. A childless couple. Whatever your reading of his identity, the pattern is the same: God comes near to people who are not looking for him, calls them by name and stays long enough to be seen.

Hagar is often noted as the first person in Scripture to give God a name, and the name she chooses is *the God who sees me*. If you are walking through fear, grief or a season of feeling unseen, that name is for you too, and it is also wise to bring that weight to a pastor, counsellor or doctor you trust.
